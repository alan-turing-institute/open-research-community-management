# Guide to the RCM paper

This guide maps the sections of the paper *Professionalising Community Management Roles in Interdisciplinary Research Projects* to practical tasks. Use it to find the part of the paper that supports your situation, then read that section for the full argument and references.

> Sharan, M., Karoune, E., Hellon, V., Gould Van Praag, C., Kayumbi, G., Bennett, A., Araujo Alvarez, A., Lee Steele, A., Batchelor, S., Lacey, A., & Whitaker, K. (2024). *Professionalising Community Management Roles in Interdisciplinary Research Projects*. arXiv:2409.00108. [https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.00108](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.00108) ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.00108.pdf))

The paper is a preprint. Section numbers below follow the arXiv v1 version.

## Defining the role

**Section 1.1: Definition of Research Community Managers.** The paper defines RCMs as professionals who foster a collaborative environment where a diverse research community can access the socio-technical infrastructure and participatory processes it needs to engage, gain recognition, and build shared agency over its work.

Practical uses:

- Borrow the three core functions when explaining the role: provide access to socio-technical infrastructure, facilitate participatory processes, and build shared ownership and agency.
- Use the note on job titles to recognise RCM work under different names, such as community coordinator, community engagement officer, or network coordinator.

## Assessing and planning a community

**Section 2.1: Community Maturation Indicator.** The indicator combines six *levels of community participation* with six *stages of community building*. A community's status is written as a "Level - Stage" pair.

| Levels of community participation | Stages of community building |
|---|---|
| 1. Inform the community | 1. Community initiation |
| 2. Invite community feedback | 2. Planning and design of community strategy |
| 3. Engage and involve members in initiatives | 3. Implementation of community strategy |
| 4. Mobilise and connect groups | 4. Growth and scaling |
| 5. Empower groups to make decisions | 5. Monitoring and evaluation to build sustainability pathways |
| 6. Decentralise decision-making power | 6. Sustaining or sunsetting the community |

Practical uses:

- Place a community on the indicator at the start of a project, then review it at regular points.
- Work through the stages at one level before moving to the next level of participation.
- Use Supplementary 1 of the paper for descriptions and examples of each combination.

**Section 2.2: Adapting strategies to maturation status.** Technical and cultural context shapes the infrastructure and practices an RCM chooses. The paper contrasts open source software projects (a permissive licence and open repository), public health data projects (trusted research environments and Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement), and participatory projects (citizen science approaches).

For the end of a community's life, see the team's [sunsetting guide](resources.md#project-and-community-lifecycle).

## Embedding best practices

**Section 2.3: Best practices that improve research quality.** The paper links RCM work to Responsible Research and Innovation (anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion, and responsiveness) and describes five areas where RCMs champion good practice:

| Section | Area | Use it to... |
|---|---|---|
| 2.3.1 | Reproducibility | Support reproducible workflows across disciplines |
| 2.3.2 | Openness and transparency | Plan open practices and documentation |
| 2.3.3 | Accountability and innovation | Define shared responsibilities and review processes |
| 2.3.4 | Equity, diversity, inclusion, and accessibility (EDIA) | Build inclusive participation into project design |
| 2.3.5 | Fair attribution and recognition | Acknowledge all contributions; see the [contributorship roles guide](resources.md#open-research-and-recognition) |

## Examples from the Turing

**Section 3: RCM roles in practice.** Section 3.1 describes projects at The Alan Turing Institute that had institutionally supported RCM roles, starting with *The Turing Way* in 2019. Supplementary 2 gives the full table of project examples.

**Section 3.2: A team-based approach.** The Turing's RCM team set six shared goals:

1. Embed open, inclusive, and reproducible research practices.
2. Ensure a shared understanding of goals, roadmap, and processes.
3. Facilitate stakeholder engagement and collaboration.
4. Provide technical support and domain expertise.
5. Co-create, maintain, and communicate project resources.
6. Amplify and champion community learnings and achievements.

Practical uses:

- Adapt the six goals when drafting goals for a community-management team. The team's own [goals](goals.md) page records how it applied them.
- Use section 3.2.1 as an example of how the team was led, supported, and housed within the Tools, Practices and Systems programme.

## Writing roles and building skills

**Section 4: RCM Skills and Competency Framework.** The framework describes 65 skills in five areas. Communication and engagement are core competencies; the other three are peripheral competencies that RCMs usually exercise in collaboration with other specialists.

| Section | Skill area | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 4.2.2.1 | Communications | Core |
| 4.2.2.2 | Engagement | Core |
| 4.2.3.1 | Strategic contributions | Peripheral |
| 4.2.3.2 | Technical expertise | Peripheral |
| 4.2.3.3 | Accountability | Peripheral |

Practical uses (section 4.2.1 lists the intended audiences):

| Audience | How to use the framework |
|---|---|
| Organisations | Establish the infrastructure needed to develop and sustain RCM roles and teams |
| Experienced community professionals | Identify skills to develop or to support in others |
| New community managers | Understand your fit with the role and plan career development |
| Individual researchers | See how RCM roles complement other specialist positions |
| Research teams | Decide which community responsibilities to share and with whom |

- Write job descriptions by prioritising the skills most important to the project's goals, and separate what the RCM does independently from what they do with colleagues such as research software engineers, project managers, or data stewards.
- Compare the framework with the Community Roundtable and CSCCE skills frameworks, which the paper summarises in Supplementary 3.2.
- Find the full skill lists in Supplementary 3.1.

## Making the case for RCM roles

**Section 4.1: The need for professionalisation.** Explains why community management has remained informal and "hidden", and what professionalisation involves: recognition, training, qualifications, autonomy, and fair compensation.

**Section 5: Recommended steps towards professionalisation.**

| Section | Step | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Articulate skills and competencies | Adopt a shared framework for role design |
| 5.2 | Align recognition and reward systems | Include community work in assessment, promotion, and authorship (see also CRediT and DORA) |
| 5.3 | Build long-term career pathways | Show how RCMs can progress to senior and leadership roles |
| 5.4 | Offer prolonged mentorship and support | Plan mentoring and an institutional support network |

**Section 6: Responding to institutional barriers.** Lists systemic barriers to community building, and describes:

- 6.1: The advantages of RCM teams over isolated roles.
- 6.2: Routes to formalise RCM skills in organisations that cannot yet hire dedicated roles, for example by using the framework in existing job descriptions.
- 6.3: Approaches to adopt from the research software engineering (RSE) movement.

**Section 7: Conclusion.** Summarises the recommendation that institutions invest in RCM teams that are empowered to prioritise collaboration, transparency, and community-based approaches.
