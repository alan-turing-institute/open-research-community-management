Guide to the RCM paper#

This guide maps the sections of the paper Professionalising Community Management Roles in Interdisciplinary Research Projects to practical tasks. Use it to find the part of the paper that supports your situation, then read that section for the full argument and references.

Sharan, M., Karoune, E., Hellon, V., Gould Van Praag, C., Kayumbi, G., Bennett, A., Araujo Alvarez, A., Lee Steele, A., Batchelor, S., Lacey, A., & Whitaker, K. (2024). Professionalising Community Management Roles in Interdisciplinary Research Projects. arXiv:2409.00108. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.00108 (PDF)

The paper is a preprint. Section numbers below follow the arXiv v1 version.

Defining the role#

Section 1.1: Definition of Research Community Managers. The paper defines RCMs as professionals who foster a collaborative environment where a diverse research community can access the socio-technical infrastructure and participatory processes it needs to engage, gain recognition, and build shared agency over its work.

Practical uses:

  • Borrow the three core functions when explaining the role: provide access to socio-technical infrastructure, facilitate participatory processes, and build shared ownership and agency.

  • Use the note on job titles to recognise RCM work under different names, such as community coordinator, community engagement officer, or network coordinator.

Assessing and planning a community#

Section 2.1: Community Maturation Indicator. The indicator combines six levels of community participation with six stages of community building. A community’s status is written as a “Level - Stage” pair.

Levels of community participation

Stages of community building

1. Inform the community

1. Community initiation

2. Invite community feedback

2. Planning and design of community strategy

3. Engage and involve members in initiatives

3. Implementation of community strategy

4. Mobilise and connect groups

4. Growth and scaling

5. Empower groups to make decisions

5. Monitoring and evaluation to build sustainability pathways

6. Decentralise decision-making power

6. Sustaining or sunsetting the community

Practical uses:

  • Place a community on the indicator at the start of a project, then review it at regular points.

  • Work through the stages at one level before moving to the next level of participation.

  • Use Supplementary 1 of the paper for descriptions and examples of each combination.

Section 2.2: Adapting strategies to maturation status. Technical and cultural context shapes the infrastructure and practices an RCM chooses. The paper contrasts open source software projects (a permissive licence and open repository), public health data projects (trusted research environments and Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement), and participatory projects (citizen science approaches).

For the end of a community’s life, see the team’s sunsetting guide.

Examples from the Turing#

Section 3: RCM roles in practice. Section 3.1 describes projects at The Alan Turing Institute that had institutionally supported RCM roles, starting with The Turing Way in 2019. Supplementary 2 gives the full table of project examples.

Section 3.2: A team-based approach. The Turing’s RCM team set six shared goals:

  1. Embed open, inclusive, and reproducible research practices.

  2. Ensure a shared understanding of goals, roadmap, and processes.

  3. Facilitate stakeholder engagement and collaboration.

  4. Provide technical support and domain expertise.

  5. Co-create, maintain, and communicate project resources.

  6. Amplify and champion community learnings and achievements.

Practical uses:

  • Adapt the six goals when drafting goals for a community-management team. The team’s own goals page records how it applied them.

  • Use section 3.2.1 as an example of how the team was led, supported, and housed within the Tools, Practices and Systems programme.

Making the case for RCM roles#

Section 4.1: The need for professionalisation. Explains why community management has remained informal and “hidden”, and what professionalisation involves: recognition, training, qualifications, autonomy, and fair compensation.

Section 5: Recommended steps towards professionalisation.

Section

Step

Practical use

5.1

Articulate skills and competencies

Adopt a shared framework for role design

5.2

Align recognition and reward systems

Include community work in assessment, promotion, and authorship (see also CRediT and DORA)

5.3

Build long-term career pathways

Show how RCMs can progress to senior and leadership roles

5.4

Offer prolonged mentorship and support

Plan mentoring and an institutional support network

Section 6: Responding to institutional barriers. Lists systemic barriers to community building, and describes:

  • 6.1: The advantages of RCM teams over isolated roles.

  • 6.2: Routes to formalise RCM skills in organisations that cannot yet hire dedicated roles, for example by using the framework in existing job descriptions.

  • 6.3: Approaches to adopt from the research software engineering (RSE) movement.

Section 7: Conclusion. Summarises the recommendation that institutions invest in RCM teams that are empowered to prioritise collaboration, transparency, and community-based approaches.
